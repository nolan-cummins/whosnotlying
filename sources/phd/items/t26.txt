@ 2188 | A Strong Opioid Without the Danger | Medicine, Chemistry | National Institute on Drug Abuse, USA
Chemists found a painkiller that activates the opioid receptor even more strongly than morphine yet causes little of the breathing suppression, tolerance and dependence that make opioids deadly.
- Opioids relieve pain by activating the mu-opioid receptor
- Their danger comes from how hard they drive that same receptor
- The usual strategy is to make weaker, partial activators
- This one is a "superagonist", driving the receptor harder than morphine
- Respiratory depression is what kills in opioid overdose
# opioids, painkillers, mu-opioid receptor, overdose, addiction, drug design

@ 2196 | Stress Hormone Scrambles Your Internal Map | Neuroscience & Psychology, Medicine | Ruhr University Bochum, Germany
Men given cortisol, the stress hormone, were worse at tracking their own position while navigating, and the grid-like pattern of brain activity that underlies that ability was disrupted.
- Path integration means keeping track of where you are by your own movement
- Grid cells in the entorhinal cortex fire in a hexagonal pattern
- That brain region carries receptors for cortisol
- It's also among the first areas damaged in Alzheimer's disease
# cortisol, stress, path integration, grid cells, entorhinal cortex, navigation

@ 2204 | A 20-Minute Psychedelic for Depression | Medicine, Neuroscience & Psychology | Imperial College London, UK & Johns Hopkins University, USA
A trial gave adults with moderate to severe depression intravenous DMT, a psychedelic whose effects last only minutes rather than hours, testing whether a short trip can still lift mood.
- DMT acts on serotonin receptors like psilocybin and LSD
- Its effects come on fast and fade within about half an hour
- Shorter sessions would need far less clinical supervision
- The trial was double-blind and placebo-controlled
# DMT, psychedelics, major depression, clinical trial, serotonin, psychiatry

@ 2237 | England's Lung Scans Catch Cancer Early | Medicine, Law & Government | NHS England, UK
Five years into England's targeted lung screening programme, over two million people have been invited and 7,193 lung cancers found, with 63% caught at the earliest stage.
- Lung cancer is usually found late, when it's hard to treat
- Screening uses low-dose CT scans of the chest
- Invitations go to 55- to 74-year-olds who have ever smoked
- A further 12.6% were found at stage 2
- The early-stage share rose most in the poorest areas
# lung cancer, screening, NHS, low-dose CT, early detection, England

@ 2241 | AI Spots Oesophageal Cancer in Ordinary Scans | Medicine, Computing & AI | Alibaba Group & Sichuan Cancer Hospital, China
An AI found precancerous changes and early oesophageal cancer in plain chest CT scans, a task long considered impossible because the oesophagus is a floppy, collapsing tube.
- Oesophageal cancer is usually found by passing a camera down the throat
- Plain CT scans are cheap and widely available
- The oesophagus collapses and moves, blurring small lesions
- Early detection greatly improves survival
# oesophageal cancer, CT screening, artificial intelligence, early detection, China

@ 2247 | AI Raises Breast Cancer Detection in Practice | Medicine, Computing & AI | University of Lübeck, Germany
Across 463,094 women screened at 12 German sites, radiologists who used AI support found 17.6% more breast cancers than those reading without it, without recalling more women unnecessarily.
- German screening has two radiologists read every mammogram
- Radiologists chose voluntarily whether to use the AI
- Detection rose from 5.7 to 6.7 cancers per 1,000 women
- Recall rate is how often women are called back for more tests
- Most earlier AI studies looked back at old scans instead
# mammography, breast cancer screening, artificial intelligence, radiology, Germany

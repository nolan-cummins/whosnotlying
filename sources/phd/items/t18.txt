@ 1464 | Shades That Open When It Rains | Architecture, 3D Printing & Manufacturing | University of Stuttgart & University of Freiburg, Germany
4D-printed shading panels made from wood cellulose curl and uncurl on their own as humidity changes, copying how pine cones open, so a building facade can shade itself without motors or power.
- 4D printing means printed objects that change shape over time
- Cellulose swells when it absorbs moisture from the air
- Two layers that swell differently make the panel bend
- Pine cones and seed pods open by the same mechanism
- The panels were tested on a real facade mock-up
# 4D printing, adaptive shading, cellulose, biomimetics, facades, humidity

@ 1473 | A Week Without Food, Same Strength | Medicine, Sports | Norwegian School of Sport Sciences, Norway
Thirteen people who fasted for seven days lost about 6 kilograms yet kept their maximum muscle strength intact, though their endurance and peak oxygen uptake fell 13%.
- They lost 4.6 kilograms of lean mass and 1.4 of fat
- Muscle glycogen, the stored fuel for hard effort, halved
- A key enzyme blocking carbohydrate burning rose 13-fold
- Maximum strength depends less on fuel stores than endurance does
- Humans historically had to work hard while short of food
# fasting, muscle strength, endurance, glycogen, metabolism, exercise physiology

@ 1487 | One Model, 100 Languages, Speech and Text | Language, Computing & AI | Meta & University of California, Berkeley, USA
Meta built a single AI model that translates speech and text across roughly 100 languages, handling speech-to-speech, speech-to-text and text-to-speech in one system rather than chaining separate tools.
- Older systems chained a speech recognizer, translator and speech synthesizer
- Errors compound at each step in a chain
- It translates speech into 36 languages and text into 96
- Most translation research has focused on a few major languages
# machine translation, speech translation, multilingual AI, SeamlessM4T, Meta

@ 1494 | Lopsided Teams Build Better Software | Computing & AI, Economics & Trade | Central European University, Austria
Analyzing the moment-by-moment history of open-source projects in Rust, JavaScript and Python showed that teams where work is most unevenly divided, with one lead developer doing most of it, tend to be more successful.
- Open-source projects log every contribution publicly
- Most teamwork studies treat teams as fixed groups
- A lead developer typically emerged early in each project
- Success was measured by project adoption and activity
# software teams, open source, leadership, collaboration, GitHub, teamwork

@ 1505 | Floods Don't Respect State Lines | Law & Government, Disasters | Columbia University, USA
Simulations of US flood insurance found that pooling risk nationally keeps the system solvent, while splitting it into state-level pools would leave many states insolvent, because big floods cross state boundaries.
- The National Flood Insurance Program covers most US flood policies
- Proposals would shift disaster management from FEMA to the states
- Insurance pools work by spreading risk across many places
- A flood hitting several states at once drains several small pools together
# flood insurance, FEMA, risk pooling, disasters, United States, policy

@ 1507 | Medical AI Leaks Some Patients More | Medicine, Computing & AI | Technical University of Munich, Germany
A patient-by-patient audit of medical AI models found privacy risk is very unevenly spread: attacks that reveal whether someone's records were used in training succeed far more often for some patients than others.
- Medical AI is trained on sensitive patient records
- A membership inference attack tests if a record was in the training set
- Earlier work only measured success averaged across a whole dataset
- Patients who contribute several similar records are more exposed
# medical AI, privacy, membership inference, patient data, machine learning

@ 1510 | Taxing Meat, Untaxing Vegetables | Food & Drink, Economics & Trade | University of Oxford, UK & University of Copenhagen, Denmark
Modelling across Europe found that raising value-added tax on meat and dairy while cutting it on fruit and vegetables would improve diets and bring health, environmental and economic gains in most countries.
- Many European countries already apply reduced VAT rates to food
- That leaves room to adjust rates up or down
- Health gains came mainly from eating more fruit and vegetables
- Meat and dairy carry the largest environmental footprints
# food taxes, value-added tax, diet, climate, Europe, public health

@ 1515 | Grain Built States, Not Farming | Ancient History, Law & Government | University of Bristol, UK & University of Auckland, New Zealand
Mapping traits of 868 cultures onto a global language family tree suggests intensive farming was as likely a result of states forming as its cause, while growing grain did come first.
- States are large societies with centralized authority
- Grain can be measured, stored and taxed easily
- Writing may have developed to record those taxes
- Bayesian methods test which trait came first across related cultures
# state formation, agriculture, grain, taxation, writing, cultural evolution

@ 1518 | Who the Global South Trusts on Climate | Weather & Climate, Law & Government | University of Gothenburg, Sweden & UC San Diego, USA
Surveys of 8,400 people across Chile, Colombia, India, Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa and Vietnam found scientists were the most trusted source on climate in six of the seven countries.
- Most climate opinion research has been done in rich countries
- Trust in scientists went with greater climate knowledge
- Respondents agreed climate change is urgent
- Support slipped when climate was weighed against other priorities
# climate attitudes, global south, trust in scientists, surveys, climate policy

@ 1522 | Teaching AI to See Like a Baby | Computing & AI, Neuroscience & Psychology | Osnabrück University & Freie Universität Berlin, Germany
Training an image AI on a "developmental visual diet", starting blurry and colourless like a newborn's vision and sharpening over time, made it rely on shape rather than texture and much harder to fool.
- AI vision usually keeps texture cues and ignores overall shape
- Human infants start with poor acuity and little colour vision
- Standard AI training shows sharp, full-colour images from the start
- The trained model resisted distortions and adversarial attacks better
# computer vision, developmental learning, shape bias, AI robustness, infant vision

@ 1616 | Europe's Wetlands Are in Fragments | Plants, Law & Government | University of Copenhagen, Denmark & University of Greifswald, Germany
Mapping six wetland types across 38 European countries from satellite images found them so broken up that roughly a third of all wetland area sits in patches smaller than 25 hectares.
- Wetlands store large amounts of carbon in waterlogged peat
- Centuries of draining for farming have shrunk and split them
- 7 to 11% of wetland area is in patches under 1 hectare
- EU law requires restoring 30% of wetlands not in good condition by 2030
- Small patches dry out and lose species more easily
# wetlands, fragmentation, Europe, restoration, peatland, satellite mapping

@ 1624 | HIV Treatment Deserts in Malawi | Medicine, Geography & Travel | UCLA & Arizona State University, USA
Mapping clinics, roads and travel modes across Malawi, where about 9% of adults have HIV, revealed "treatment deserts" where the nearest facility is effectively out of reach on foot.
- Antiretroviral therapy must be taken continuously for life
- Many patients walk, bike or pay for transport to collect medicine
- Access depends on travel time and on how many patients each clinic serves
- The model combined clinic locations with population demand
# HIV, antiretroviral therapy, healthcare access, Malawi, geospatial modelling

@ 1626 | Humans Learned to Live Anywhere Before Leaving Africa | Ancient History, Geography & Travel | University of Cambridge, UK & Max Planck Institute of Geoanthropology, Germany
Before the migration that peopled all of Eurasia about 50,000 years ago, humans in Africa had expanded into a much wider range of habitats, from deserts to rainforests, than their ancestors occupied.
- All living non-Africans descend mostly from that one later wave
- Fossils show earlier migrations that left little genetic trace
- The team assembled a pan-African database of dated archaeological sites
- Niche breadth means the range of environments a population can use
- Flexibility may be why the later dispersal succeeded where earlier ones failed
# human evolution, out of Africa, niche breadth, archaeology, dispersal

@ 1635 | Alpine Plants Going Locally Extinct | Plants, Weather & Climate | University of Vienna & Austrian Academy of Sciences, Austria
Resurveying 896 fixed plots on 62 European mountain summits over 21 years showed local plant extinctions rising, fastest where warming was strongest, even as total species counts went up.
- Warming lets lowland species move up mountains, raising counts
- That masks the quiet loss of cold-adapted residents
- Species vanished most at the warm, low edge of their range
- Thermophilization means a community shifting toward warmth-loving species
- Extinctions followed years of declining abundance
# alpine plants, extinction, climate change, mountain summits, Europe, biodiversity

@ 1637 | A Quarter of US Glaciers Are Gone | Earth Science, Weather & Climate | Portland State University & US Geological Survey, USA
The first full survey of glaciers in the lower 48 US states found about a quarter have disappeared since the mid-twentieth century, and the rest have lost a third of their area and volume.
- These glaciers feed alpine streams in late summer
- Most are small, so local shade and snowdrift matter more than climate alone
- Their area and volume change had never been comprehensively measured
- Losing them changes river flows and water temperature
# glaciers, United States, glacier loss, alpine hydrology, climate change

@ 1640 | Climate Threatens Nearly Every Island | Geography & Travel, Weather & Climate | CNRS & AgroParisTech, France
Assessing invasive species, land-use change and climate change for over 16,000 islands worldwide found climate change is the one threat that reaches almost all of them by 2050.
- Islands hold a disproportionate share of the world's unique species
- Global threat maps are usually too coarse to show islands at all
- Invasive rats, cats and plants devastate island wildlife
- The assessment combined several threat indicators for each island
# islands, biodiversity, invasive species, climate change, conservation, threats

@ 1644 | Stone Age Sailors Reached Malta | Ancient History, Geography & Travel | Max Planck Institute of Geoanthropology, Germany & University of Malta
Hunter-gatherers reached the remote Maltese islands about 8,500 years ago, a thousand years before farming arrived, crossing roughly 100 kilometres of open sea from Sicily.
- Malta is among the most isolated island groups in the Mediterranean
- Small islands were thought too poor in resources for foragers
- A land bridge to Sicily had drowned about 13,000 years ago
- Evidence came from dates, tools, animal bones and plant remains
- They would have had no sails, only paddled craft
# Malta, hunter-gatherers, seafaring, Mediterranean, archaeology, islands

@ 1656 | Bennu's Samples Are Rich in Ammonia | Space & Astronomy, Chemistry | NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, USA
Samples returned from asteroid Bennu hold more carbon, nitrogen and ammonia than those from asteroid Ryugu or almost any meteorite, giving a clean look at early solar system chemistry.
- Meteorites are contaminated by Earth's air, water and life on landing
- Bennu's samples were sealed in space and opened in clean rooms
- Ammonia is a key ingredient for making amino acids
- Nitrogen isotopes suggest the material formed in very cold regions
# Bennu, OSIRIS-REx, ammonia, organic matter, asteroids, origin of life

@ 1664 | Radio Flashes From a White Dwarf Pair | Space & Astronomy, Physics | University of Sydney, Australia & ASTRON, Netherlands
Astronomers traced a set of mysterious repeating cosmic radio flashes to a pair of stars containing a white dwarf, with the pulses arriving once per orbit.
- Newly found radio transients repeat every few minutes to an hour
- They were thought to come from neutron stars or white dwarfs
- A white dwarf is the dense, burnt-out core of a dead star
- Pulses timed to the orbit point to interaction between the two stars
# radio transients, white dwarfs, binary stars, radio astronomy, pulses

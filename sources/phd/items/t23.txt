@ 1961 | Archaea Make Bacteria-Killing Weapons | Microbiology, Chemistry | Imperial College London, UK
Archaea, the third great branch of life, turn out to carry enzymes that chew through bacterial cell walls, the first clear evidence they attack bacteria deliberately.
- Archaea are single-celled microbes distinct from bacteria
- Bacterial cell walls are built from a mesh called peptidoglycan
- Archaea's own walls are made differently, so the enzyme can't hurt them
- Little is known about how archaea interact socially
# archaea, bacteria, peptidoglycan, microbial warfare, enzymes, microbiology

@ 1968 | Soil Microbes React Predictably to Extremes | Microbiology, Weather & Climate | University of Manchester & UK Centre for Ecology & Hydrology
Soils from 30 European grasslands, put through drought, flood, freezing and heat under identical lab conditions, showed consistent and predictable shifts in their microbe communities.
- Soil microbes drive the cycling of carbon and nutrients
- Extreme weather is becoming more frequent
- Each soil came from a different climate and history
- Predictable responses make ecosystem effects easier to forecast
# soil microbiome, climate extremes, drought, grasslands, biogeochemistry

@ 1992 | Penguins Ride the Tide Home | Birds, Marine Life | Max Planck Institute of Animal Behavior, Germany & CONICET, Argentina
GPS tracking of 27 Magellanic penguins showed they let tidal currents carry them sideways on the way home rather than fighting them, taking a longer route but saving effort and finding food on the way.
- Tidal currents can push a swimmer far off a straight line
- Dead-reckoning loggers record heading and speed between GPS fixes
- Fighting a current costs a lot of energy
- Drifting routes passed through productive feeding water
# Magellanic penguins, navigation, tidal currents, foraging, GPS tracking

@ 2000 | Polar Bear Fur Resists Ice | Animals, Materials Science | University of Bergen, Norway & Trinity College Dublin, Ireland
Ice sticks to polar bear fur about as weakly as to a non-stick fluorocarbon coating, and the reason is the greasy sebum coating each hair.
- Polar bears are the only Arctic land mammal that dives to hunt
- Their fur is usually seen free of ice despite the cold
- Washing the grease out made ice stick far harder
- The sebum contains cholesterol and specific fatty compounds
# polar bears, anti-icing, fur, sebum, biomimetics, materials science

@ 2004 | Hibernating Bears' Muscles Idle | Animals, Medicine | University of Padua, Italy
Muscle fibres taken from the same brown bears in winter and summer showed that during hibernation their resting muscle burns much less energy, helping explain why they barely waste away.
- People lose muscle fast during weeks of bed rest
- Bears hibernate for months yet keep most of their muscle
- Even relaxed muscle constantly consumes energy
- The motor protein myosin used less fuel in hibernation
- Force production declined only mildly
# hibernation, bears, muscle wasting, myosin, metabolism, ATP

@ 2007 | The Rich Drive the Extremes | Weather & Climate, Economics & Trade | IIASA, Austria & Humboldt University of Berlin, Germany
Emissions from the consumption and investments of the wealthiest 10% of people are responsible for two-thirds of global warming since 1990, and the top 1% for a fifth of it.
- The analysis covered 1990 to 2020
- It linked emissions inequality to regional climate extremes
- Investments as well as personal consumption were counted
- Those least responsible often suffer the worst impacts
# climate inequality, emissions, wealth, climate extremes, climate justice

@ 2022 | Flying Bats Replay Their Routes | Animals, Neuroscience & Psychology | University of California, Berkeley & HHMI, USA
Wireless recordings from the brains of freely flying fruit bats caught them replaying whole flight paths during rest, both forwards and in reverse, compressed into a fraction of the real time.
- Replay was known in rats running mazes, but not in flying animals
- The hippocampus stores memories of places and routes
- The bats flew and foraged freely, not along forced paths
- Replay coincided with sharp bursts of brain activity
# hippocampus, replay, bats, flight, memory, navigation, neuroscience

@ 2029 | Two Fungi Behind White-Nose Disease | Animals, Microbiology | Université de Montpellier & IRD, France
The disease that has killed millions of North American bats turns out to be caused by two distinct fungal species, each specialized on different bat hosts, not one as assumed.
- White-nose disease has one of the highest death tolls recorded in wild mammals
- The fungus grows on hibernating bats' skin, rousing them to starve
- Research had focused on hosts and environment, not pathogen variation
- Host-specialized species may need different control strategies
# white-nose syndrome, bats, fungal disease, wildlife disease, pathogen genetics

@ 2030 | Bats Hunt Faster With the Lights On | Animals, Physics | Leibniz Institute for Zoo and Wildlife Research, Germany
Wild bats caught insects faster under artificial light than in darkness, combining what they saw with their echolocation rather than relying on sound alone.
- Bats navigate and hunt mainly by echolocation
- They have kept working eyes despite the cost of maintaining them
- Prey capture happens in split seconds
- Combining senses can improve fast decisions
# bats, echolocation, vision, multisensory integration, artificial light, hunting

@ 2032 | Lizards Catch Up on Lost Sleep | Animals, Neuroscience & Psychology | Hokkaido University & Nagoya University, Japan
Sleep-deprived Australian bearded dragons showed a rebound in slow brain waves afterwards, the signature of sleep being regulated as a need, in a brain region quite unlike the mammalian cortex.
- Mammals and birds show slow-wave and REM-like sleep stages
- Rebound after deprivation is the classic test of sleep regulation
- The lizard's slow waves arise in the dorsal ventricular ridge
- That structure is not a cortex, yet behaves like one for sleep
# sleep, lizards, bearded dragon, slow waves, sleep homeostasis, brain evolution

@ 2034 | Turtles Steer by Compass, Then Correct | Animals, Geography & Travel | Deakin University, Australia & Swansea University, UK
A new satellite tag that relays an animal's compass heading showed green turtles crossing oceans hold a fixed heading for long stretches, then reorient mid-ocean when they drift off course.
- The migrations covered over 1,000 kilometres and averaged 27.5 days
- Turtles return to specific nesting and feeding sites
- A fixed heading drifts off target in ocean currents
- How animals navigate open ocean has been debated for a century
# green turtles, navigation, compass heading, migration, satellite tags, oceans
